State of Alabama v. Kathy Diane Birge

A husband dies at home, is flown to Indiana and buried. Twenty days later he is exhumed. His wife is convicted of capital murder, and then the conviction is thrown out over who carried the blood samples to the lab.

The case in one paragraph

Cecil Birge died at his home on May 5, 2001, in what first looked like a natural death. His body was embalmed that evening, and four days later it was flown to Indiana and buried in the plot beside his son. When investigators learned that his wife, Kathy Diane Birge, had forged his signature on a will naming her the primary beneficiary, his daughter agreed to an exhumation. An autopsy 20 days after the death, relying on an outside lab's toxicology report, concluded he died of an overdose of multiple prescription drugs. An Alabama jury convicted Kathy Birge of capital murder for pecuniary gain and she was sentenced to life without parole. On May 25, 2007, the Alabama Court of Criminal Appeals reversed the murder conviction and ordered a new trial: the State had never shown who handled the samples between the morgue and the lab.

People

  • Cecil Birge: the husband. Died at home May 5, 2001.
  • Kathy Diane Birge: the wife and defendant. Pleaded guilty to second-degree forgery; convicted at trial of capital murder and first-degree theft.
  • Michelle Swift: Cecil's daughter. Testified he was buried next to her brother; gave permission to exhume.
  • Dr. John Pless: professor of pathology, Indiana University School of Medicine. Performed the autopsy.
  • Gustave Etame: Dr. Pless's autopsy assistant, the last identified person to handle the samples. He did not testify.
  • Sheriff Mike Blakely, Deputy District Attorney Brian Jones, Investigator Bobby Smith: travelled to Indiana for the exhumation and autopsy.
  • Judge Shaw: wrote the appeals court opinion. Baschab, McMillan and Welch concurred; Wise concurred in the result.

What the State said happened

Everything in this section is the prosecution's evidence as the appeals court summarized it. The murder conviction built on it was reversed.

  • She had access to the prescription drugs found in his body, gave them to him secretly, and refused to get medical help when he became ill.
  • She had talked with a friend about hiring a "hit man" or killing him with a drug overdose to collect his life insurance.
  • She changed his will to make herself the primary beneficiary, forged his signature on it, and changed his employee-benefits account the same way.
  • She told people he was gravely ill with cancer, heart disease and colon polyps. The State's evidence was that he was not ill in the days before he died.
  • She told the Limestone County coroner she knew of no medications he was taking and did not want an autopsy, after telling others he took a lot of medication and drank heavily.
  • She insisted he wanted to be cremated. He had told his daughter he wanted to be buried in Indiana next to his son.
  • She gave conflicting accounts of where and how she found the body.
  • A computer from her home held files showing visits to websites on arsenic poisoning, venomous animals and poisonous plants. A computer from her business showed searches on estate planning, probate and wills.

The turning point: three missing days

Dr. Pless could describe drawing the samples, labelling them and watching his assistant lock them in the morgue refrigerator. After that, nothing. He did not know which courier took them, who received them at the lab, who ran the tests, or who calibrated the instruments. The assistant never testified. The lab report was unsigned. Asked for names, Dr. Pless answered, "No, but I can certainly get them for you."

Alabama law (Ex parte Holton, 1991) treats every person who handles evidence as a link, and a link with no proof at all is a "missing link" that makes the evidence inadmissible. The court found several, and noted the toxicology was "the very crux of the State's case." Without it Dr. Pless could describe the autopsy but could not say what drugs were in the body or name a cause of death. "We do not reverse a capital-murder conviction lightly," the court wrote, and reversed.

The defence lost one argument worth a line in a script: the State did not have to account for the body between the house, the funeral home and the grave. The chain only starts when the State takes custody, which here was at the graveside.

Angles for an episode

  • The cremation that didn't happen. A cremation contract bought three days early, a daughter who knew he wanted a burial, and a burial that preserved the evidence.
  • Paper before poison. The case opened as a forgery investigation. The will is what put investigators at the grave.
  • A holiday weekend. Samples drawn on a Friday, logged the following Tuesday, with no witness for the days between.
  • Guilty of forging the will, not guilty (yet) of the death. The forgery plea and theft conviction stood while the murder verdict fell.

Not yet verified: check before you record

  • What happened on remand. The opinion orders a new trial. Whether she was retried, pleaded, or the charge was dropped is not in this record. Start with the Limestone County Circuit Court file and the News Courier (Athens, Ala.) archive.
  • Trial date and trial judge. Not stated in the opinion.
  • The friend in the "hit man" conversation, the insurance amount, Cecil's age and job. Not named in the opinion; the trial transcript would have them.
  • The May 2 date is counted back from "three days before his death."
